    Section 1.2 Examples

    Introduction.

    What you will learn explained through two examples... Purposes of data analysis: exploration, inference, and prediction. Most of the time we’re trying to answer a specific research question, but sometimes we just want to explore the data and see what sort of patterns and relationships emerge. This exploratin can be used to generate new research questions, too.
    Terms in this section, and indeed much of this chapter, will all be revisited later in the text. The plan for now is simply to get a sense of the role statistics can play in practice.

    Subsection 1.2.1 First Example - Breaking Down the Process of a Statistical Investigation

    This first example is of a statistical investigation that was performed to answer a specific research question.

    Question 1.2.1. Can you relate to this scenario?

    You’re feeling tired, but you’re in the middle of something and don’t want to or can’t take a break. So you reach for a pick me up, such as a cup of coffee, tea, or maybe an energy drink.
    Now discuss:
    • Did it help?
    • Were you able to complete your task?
    • Complete it well?
    • Do you think taking a nap, even a short 10-minute nap, would have been helpful?
    • More or less helpful than drinking caffeine?

    Investigation 1.2.1. Sleep or Caffeine for Memory?

    In this investigation, reseachers ansered the question: How does caffeine compare to napping in terms of verbal, motor, and perceptual memory? [4] This is a link to the published research article.
     1 
    pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2603066/
    (a) What we already know… and what we still wonder about….
    The first step of any statistical investigation is to develop a research question. The researchers observed that there has been an increase in the amount of caffeine that people consume and that people consume caffeine to help offset not getting enough sleep or even as a substitute for sleep. They searched the research literature and found studies reporting that caffeine enhances mood, alertness, attention, and speed of information processing.
    But they still wondered: What is the affect of caffeine on memory?
    The researchers also found in the literature that naps, like caffeine, enhance alertness and attention. But they found that naps also help with some forms of memory consolidation.
    So, they also wondered: How does the effect of caffeine on memory compare to the effect of sleep on memory?
    (b) What did the researchers discover in their own research?
    They found that caffeine impaired memory, while a nap improved memory.
    “In this study, we find that a moderate dose of caffeine impaired motor sequence learning and declarative verbal memory compared to placebo and daytime sleep. These decreases were found despite the fact that caffeine increased subjective alertness, suggesting that the caffeine dose was sufficiently high to have some psychoactive effect... An afternoon nap, on the other hand, improved free recall memory relative to the caffeine group after both a 20min and a 7hr retention interval and produced greater learning on a motor sequence task than caffeine...In addition to enhancing memory consolidation for previously studied words, naps improved the ability to learn a new list of words post-intervention when compared with caffeine.”
    (c) How did the researchers get here? What allowed them to make these conclusions?
    The short answer is that they collected data and then analyzed it! The longer answer involves a series of questions that they had to answer:
    Data Collection Questions:
    • What kind of data should be collected?
    • How do we ethically get this data?
    • Who should the data be collected from?
    • How do these decisions impact the quality and type of conclusions that can be made?
    Data Analysis Questions:
    • Lots of analysis methods exist! Which one(s) should be used?
      • How can we “see” the data to idenitfy patterns, differences, similarities, oddities, etc.
      • What numerical summaries should be computed, e.g., mean, median, mode?
      • What inferene methods or statistical models will help answer the research question?
    • What do the results of the analyses actually tell us?
    • What do the results of the analyses not tell us?
    • What follow up analyses would be helpful?
    Let’s take a closer looks at how did the researchers compared the effects of caffeine and a daytime nap on declarative verbal memory, specifically.
    (i) Data Collection Details:.
    The researchers recruited “61 adults between ages 18-39 with no personal history of neurological, psychological or other chronic illness (non-smoking) that gave informed consent to participate in the experiment……”
    Question 1.2.2. Consider This!
    Based on what you now know about the study participants, discuss: how does this affect who you apply the researcher’s finding to. Should they apply to you? How about your parents? friends? What about people who are older than 70? People who smoke? People with chronic illnesses? Is there other information you would like to know about the participants?
    For verbal memory, they tested both the participants recall and recognition abilities, and they did this for two different time intervals - 20 minutes and 7 hours. We’ll focus on just recall. Here’s how the experiement, which took place over the course of one day, went:
    1. In the morning, participants were read a list of 24 unrelated words. They heard the list 3 times. Then after 20 minutes, they were asked to recall as many words from the list as possible. The researchers recorded the number of words correctly recalled.
    2. In the afternoon, partipants were randomly assigned to either take a 90-minute nap, take a 200mg caffeine pill, or to take a placebo pill. Both pills were unmarked and looked the same.
      200mg in perspective.
      Info on caffeine in beverages <aside>.
    3. When the nappers were done sleeping, the other particpants took their pills. Then one hour later, all participants were tested for recall again. At this point, 7 hours had elapsed.
    4. Afterwards, the entire verbal memory recall test was repeated with a new list of 24 words.
    Question 1.2.3. What do you know about experiments?
    This was an experiment. What does that mean exactly? How would you define an experiment? What are other options for studies? What is a placebo? Why do you think the researchers included a placebo group in this study?
    The researchers noted that some participants did not understand the test, which led to unusable data. For the verbal recall test, they ended up with usable data from only 35 participants: 12 nappers, 12 caffeine takers, and 11 placebo takers.
